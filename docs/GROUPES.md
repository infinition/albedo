# Groupes: finding the parts of a mesh that never had any

How Albedo answers "which bits of this model are one thing" on files where
nothing in the file admits there is more than one thing, and why the answer is a
slider rather than a button.

Legend: **[x]** done and measured, **[~]** in progress, **[ ]** planned.

---

## The problem this exists for

A mesh generated by Hunyuan3D, Meshy or Tripo arrives as **one shell, one
material, one UV atlas**. Nothing separates the ground from the rocks sitting on
it, the skin from the shirt, the handle from the blade. Every downstream job —
assigning separate materials, isolating a part topologically, retopologising one
piece on its own — starts with a segmentation that no local tool provides in a
form you can look at and argue with.

Albedo was already half way there without knowing it.
[`retopo-core/src/lib.rs`](../src-tauri/crates/retopo-core/src/lib.rs) names
segmentation as a downstream member of the family, and
[`adjacency.rs`](../src-tauri/crates/retopo-core/src/adjacency.rs) already
computes **signed** dihedral angles with a comment saying the segmentation step
reads them. The slot was reserved.

## Where this sits against the literature

The field is neural, and none of it fits in a five megabyte executable that also
has to start fast enough to draw an Explorer thumbnail:

| Method | What it needs |
| --- | --- |
| [PartField](https://github.com/nv-tlabs/PartField) (ICCV 2025) | PyTorch, a GPU, trained weights |
| [P3-SAM](https://github.com/Tencent-Hunyuan/Hunyuan3D-Part) | idem, 3.7 M meshes of training |
| [SAMesh](https://github.com/gtangg12/samesh) | SAM2 multi-view, lifted to 3D |

What is here instead is the classical stack those papers benchmark against,
which is closer than the publication dates suggest: on the
[Princeton benchmark](https://segeval.cs.princeton.edu/public/paper.pdf) the
Shape Diameter Function scores 0.175 against Normalized Cuts' 0.172 while
costing a thirty-fifth as much.

Two departures from it, both forced by the input:

- **Colour is a first class feature, and the strongest one.** No classical
  benchmark uses it, because benchmark meshes are untextured. On the input this
  mode exists for, shell, material and UV island are *constant across the entire
  model* and carry exactly zero information. What actually separates the ground
  from the rocks is that somebody painted them differently.
- **The output is a hierarchy, not a partition.** Borrowed from PartField's final
  stage, whose paper notes that agglomerating over face adjacency gives crisper
  borders than clustering features independently. It is what makes the result
  something you steer rather than something you re-run.

The neural route stays open from the other end: a per-face label array produced
by any of those models outside Albedo drops into `adopt()` and gets the same
display, the same sliders and the same split.

## How it works

### Six features, of two kinds

Four are **identities** — shell, material, UV island — which can only be equal or
not, so they are barriers. Two are **measurements** — face normal and surface
colour — which have a distance, so they are costs. Confusing the two is how a
segmenter ends up with a material weight nobody can tune.

Concave and convex do **not** share a weight. The gap between two fingers is
concave, and so is the line where a rock meets the ground; that is the boundary
a segmenter exists to find. A ridge along the top of the same rock is convex and
usually runs through the *middle* of a part.

Two traps in the colour feature, both of which produce plausible wrong answers:

- `Image::texel` returns `u8 / 255.0` with **no sRGB decode**. That is the right
  number for a bake writing the same encoding back out, and the wrong number to
  take a difference of: sRGB spends half its range on the darkest fifth of the
  light, so every distance would be wrong by a factor that varies with
  brightness. Decoded to linear before OkLab.
- One bilinear tap per triangle is noise, not a colour. Six barycentric samples,
  all kept clear of the edges — a sample on a triangle border is a sample on a
  UV island border, where the neighbouring chart's pixels were bled outwards on
  purpose.

### Two stages

**Superfaces** collapse every edge nobody could argue about, on a pair of
tolerances rather than a budget: below a just-noticeable colour difference and
below a few degrees of fold, two triangles are the same surface and no weighting
anybody chooses later should change that.

> This was a threshold on the weighted cost first, and it was meaningless in two
> directions at once — raising the colour weight silently tightened it, and on a
> real textured model the ordinary grain of a photograph already exceeded it. A
> 703,907 triangle mesh produced 630,474 superfaces, which is the stage not
> running at all.

**The hierarchy** then merges superfaces cheapest-pair-first and records *every*
merge rather than stopping at a count. Cutting that order after `n - k` merges
gives exactly `k` groups with nothing recomputed.

Three things that bite:

- Barriers make the dendrogram a **forest**, so the fewest groups reachable is
  `super_count - merges.len()`, not one.
- A lazy heap re-prices pairs after each merge and can push one cheaper than a
  pair already popped, so the cost column is monotonised on the way out. The
  group-count slider is unaffected — a prefix is exactly the greedy state — but a
  threshold read off a jagged column would cut in the wrong place.
- **The engine answers in the file's triangle numbering, not its own.** The
  reader drops degenerate faces on import, which silently renumbers everything
  after the first one dropped. See below.

### The slider, and why it is live

The dendrogram travels to the front end. Moving the slider replays a prefix of
the merge order through a union-find: no geometry touched, no attribute
rewritten, nothing asked of the engine.

The result is uploaded as a **superface → group lookup texture**, not written
into a vertex attribute. On a half-million triangle model the attribute is six
megabytes and would be re-pushed on every frame of a drag, for a change whose
actual content is a few thousand numbers. Outlines are not stored either: `aNbr`
gives the superface on each side of every edge, both go through the same lookup,
and an edge whose two sides land on different groups is a border. They re-draw
themselves because the answer they read changed.

`uGroupView` is deliberately **not** a state of `uView`. That one is a single
slot the Retopo mode resets on any click in the channel strip, so a group overlay
riding on it would go dark the moment somebody looked at a roughness map.

### Two questions, not one

The hierarchy only ever produces **connected** parts. Two rocks lying apart on
the same ground share no edge, so no merging will ever join them — the floor of
the slider is the number of connected pieces and that is topology, not a setting.

Wanting one rock *material* rather than two hundred rock *objects* is the other
question, asked of the parts' mean colours rather than of what they touch. It is
answered above the engine, in the same OkLab units as the pre-merge tolerance so
the two numbers mean the same thing. Cheapest pair first, with each merge
re-checked against the two families' current means — without that re-check it is
single linkage, which chains, and a run of two hundred slightly different greys
collapses entirely.

### The split

One mesh per group, beside the one it came from, under the same parent and with
the same transform. The outliner, the visibility eyes, the gizmo, the GLB export
and retopology per part are all written against ordinary scene objects, so a
split gets every one of them for free.

Textures are **shared, not copied**: a material clone copies map references, not
the images behind them, so ten parts off one atlas cost one atlas on the GPU and
come out pixel-identical. Giving each part a tight atlas of its own is a
different operation — it is a bake, and there is already an engine here for that.

One material per group, shared across every mesh the group turns out to touch,
because a group spread over two hundred separate rocks should not hand back two
hundred materials called *Famille 1* to edit two hundred times.

## The bug worth writing down

The engine reported **900,290** triangles for a mesh the viewer had sent with
**900,328**. The mode refused to paint rather than produce a result shifted by
thirty-eight triangles.

The cause is `Mesh::remove_degenerate`, which drops zero-area faces on import
because they have no normal, break the BVH's surface area heuristic and turn
quadric errors into NaN. That is correct, and it renumbers every triangle after
the first one dropped.

It was not an isolated case. Of four test models, **three carried degenerate
faces** (2, 18 and 2), so three segmentations out of four would have been
silently misaligned. The mesh now records where its triangles came from, and the
per-face answers are expanded back to the file's numbering before being written;
dropped triangles get "no group", which the shader already leaves alone.

The lesson is the guard, not the fix. It cost four lines — compare two counts —
and it caught a class of error that renders perfectly.

## What is known and not fixed

- **WebP atlases are not decoded.** Meshy writes them, and the reader does PNG
  and JPEG. The application path is unaffected — the scene reaches the engine
  through a three.js export that writes PNG, measured — but the command line
  refuses those files, by name and with a way out rather than with "unsupported
  image encoding".
- **A model with no colour texture** collapses to one family at any tolerance,
  because every superface carries its material's flat colour. That is correct
  and it looks like a fault, so the panel says so.
- **Above 2,500 parts** the family control gives up: the pairing is quadratic,
  and past that point "which of these look alike" is not a question anybody can
  act on. The answer is to ask for fewer parts first.
